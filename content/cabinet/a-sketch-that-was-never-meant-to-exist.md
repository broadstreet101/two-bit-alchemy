## Approved Publication Draft

# A Sketch That Was Never Meant to Exist

There are a handful of things hanging on the walls of my office that probably don't mean much to anyone else.

This is one of them.

At first glance, it's just a drawing: two Amish zombies sketched in black ink by Charlie Adlard, the artist whose work defined *The Walking Dead* comic for years.

But that's not really why it hangs there.

It hangs there because of what happened before it existed.

I grew up at exactly the right time to develop a lifelong fascination with zombies. My friends and I watched VHS copies of *Night of the Living Dead*, *Dawn of the Dead*, *Day of the Dead*, and just about every second-rate imitation we could get our hands on. This would have been sometime in the mid-to-late 1980s, and I was almost certainly too young when I got my first taste of George Romero's undead.

It didn't seem to hurt anything.

By around 2005, I had started reading *The Walking Dead*. When the television adaptation arrived a few years later, I was one of the comic readers wondering whether they could possibly take this story we loved and translate it to television without screwing it up.

At least in the beginning, I thought they did an incredibly good job.

Somewhere in the long stretch between watching zombie movies as a chubby kid and watching *The Walking Dead* on television, I had also developed an idea for a tattoo.

Amish zombies.

I grew up in Appalachian Pennsylvania. There weren't a tremendous number of actual Amish immediately around my hometown, mostly Mennonite and German Baptist, but Amish country was close enough that the idea felt appropriately connected to home. More importantly, it seemed unlikely that everybody else in the world already had an Amish zombie tattoo.

I tried sketching a few ideas myself.

Nothing worked.

Eventually I had another idea.

Why not ask the guy who actually draws *The Walking Dead*?

Charlie Adlard's contact information was available through the comic's publisher, so I sent him an email. I explained that I was a huge fan, that I was considering this ridiculous Amish zombie tattoo, and that rather than asking some tattoo artist who didn't know me to invent it from scratch, I thought it couldn't hurt to ask Charlie what **his** version of Amish zombies might look like.

I wasn't asking for a finished commission. I wasn't expecting him to spend hours drawing something for a random guy who had appeared in his inbox.

As best I can remember, I told him I would be absolutely enthralled with a quick sketch on a cocktail napkin.

And somehow, he answered.

Charlie was in London with a layover and some time to kill. He said he'd see what he could do.

I had offered to pay him for his time. He declined and casually suggested that I make a donation somewhere instead.

So I donated $75 to the American Cancer Society in his name.

Then I let it go.

I had no idea whether anything would actually come of it, and frankly, getting a personal response from an artist whose work I admired was already more than I had expected.

A few weeks later, a large envelope arrived with a return address in the United Kingdom.

Inside wasn't a cocktail napkin.

It was an actual page from the sketchbook Charlie traveled with.

And on it were my Amish zombies.

Two of them, standing together in that unmistakable black-and-white *Walking Dead* world, rendered by the same hand responsible for countless pages I had spent years reading.

He signed it.

I framed it.

And ultimately, I never got the tattoo.

As much as I loved the drawing, it didn't translate into the tattoo I had imagined quite as well as I'd hoped. That turned out not to matter very much.

I had started with a weird tattoo idea and an email that I had no particular reason to believe would ever be answered.

What came back was considerably better: an original piece of artwork from someone whose work I greatly admired, created because I asked politely, he happened to have some time to kill, and a complete stranger decided to be extraordinarily generous with it.

It has been hanging on my wall ever since.
